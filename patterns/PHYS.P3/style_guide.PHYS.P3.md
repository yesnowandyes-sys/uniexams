# PHYS.P3 (Mechanics) Style Guide

## Question Structure Patterns
Questions in this domain are strictly **Single-Select Multiple Choice**. The structure follows a recognizable "Setup -> Data -> Target" format:

1.  **Contextual Setup**: A real-world scenario (helicopters, cars, fairground rides, falling objects) is described. This is rarely a "plug-and-chug" problem; it requires identifying the relevant physical model (e.g., "A car accelerates... implies $suvat$ or $F=ma$").
2.  **Data Injection**:
    *   **Values**: Masses, velocities, coefficients, and dimensions ($g$ is always provided, usually $10$ or $9.81$).
    *   **Visuals**: Heavy reliance on diagrams (graphs of velocity-time, force-distance, or physical setups like pulleys and slopes).
    *   **Conditions**: "Frictionless," "Negligible mass," "Constant speed," "Light inextensible rope."
3.  **Target**: The question asks for **two** distinct quantities in a significant portion of items (approx 40-50%).
    *   Example: "What is the velocity... and how much energy is lost?"
    *   Example: "What is the tension... and what is the force on the support?"
    *   *Consequence*: Options A-H are usually pairs of values (e.g., `A. 20, 25000`).

## Difficulty Calibration
Difficulty is not achieved by obscure calculus, but by **conceptual layering** and **process management**:

*   **Band 1-3 (Low)**: Direct application of one formula.
    *   *Example*: $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ where all variables are given.
    *   *Example*: $W = Fd$.
*   **Band 4-6 (Medium)**: Two-step processes or simple vector addition.
    *   *Example*: Resolving forces on a slope ($F=ma$).
    *   *Example*: Conservation of Momentum where $v_1$ and $v_2$ are unknowns to be solved simultaneously.
    *   *Example*: Using the area under a curve to find a physical quantity (e.g., Area of $v-t$ graph = distance).
*   **Band 7-9 (High)**: Multi-stage modeling involving efficiency, systems of masses, or energy transformations.
    *   *Example*: Hydroelectric dam problem (Band 9). Requires: $Power = \frac{Energy}{Time} \rightarrow Energy = mgh \rightarrow Volume = \frac{mass}{density}$, incorporating efficiency ($0.9$).
    *   *Example*: P3.2/3.3 integration: "Tension in the coupling" requires summing forces (Helicopter + Crate) rather than just looking at the crate.

## Wording Conventions
*   **Precise Vector Language**: "Speed" (scalar) vs "Velocity" (vector). "Distance" vs "Displacement". If direction changes, "distance" is total path length.
*   **Assumptions**: Usually explicitly stated ("air resistance can be ignored"). If not stated for a projectile, it is still standard to ignore it unless "terminal velocity" is mentioned.
*   **Units**: SI units are standard. However, watch for mixed units in density questions (e.g., $\text{g cm}^{-3}$ vs $\text{kg m}^{-3}$).
*   **Value of $g$**: Almost always provided in the stem (e.g., "$g = 10 \text{ N kg}^{-1}$"). Do not assume $9.81$ if not given.

## Calculator-Free Arithmetic Patterns
Since this is a calculator-free section, numbers are designed for **mental math**:

1.  ** $g = 10$ **: The vast majority of questions use $g=10$. $9.81$ is rare and usually paired with simple numbers ($100$ or $1000$).
2.  **Pythagorean Triples**: Angles and forces often use $3-4-5$ triangles.
    *   *Example*: $37^\circ$ and $53^\circ$ approximations ($\sin 37 \approx 0.6$, $\cos 37 \approx 0.8$).
    *   *Example*: Right-angled force triangles with sides $30, 40, 50$.
3.  **Fractional Manipulation**: Momentum questions often involve mass ratios like $4:5$ or $2:1$ to keep algebra clean.
4.  **Powers of 10**: Large numbers (e.g., density of water $1000$, speed of light $3 \times 10^8$) are used to test standard form handling rather than multiplication skills.
5.  **Common Percentage Fractions**: Efficiencies are often $50\%$, $75\%$ ($3/4$), or $80\%$ ($4/5$). Students must be comfortable inverting these (e.g., $Output = 0.8 \times Input \iff Input = Output / 0.8$).
